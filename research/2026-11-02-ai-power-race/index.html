Why Is the Next AI Race Being Won by Power Companies, Not Chipmakers?

Megawatts on a date now decide where AI capacity gets built

2 Nov 2026 · By The Signal

Signal Research Notes is an original research series on The Signal, published three times a week. Each edition asks one question, works through it from primary sources, and keeps confirmed facts separate from analysis. Sources are linked at the end.

For two years the AI infrastructure story was a chip story: who could buy the most accelerators, and who could ship them fastest. In sixteen days, from September 30 to October 15, 2026, the biggest AI infrastructure announcements were power contracts, not chip orders. Amazon, Google, and an American gas exporter signed deals whose real subject was megawatts on a date, and that constraint now decides where AI capacity gets built.

1. Sixteen days of power deals, and what each one actually buys

On September 30, Constellation Energy and Amazon announced a 20 year power purchase agreement covering 690 megawatts from the Calvert Cliffs nuclear plant in Maryland. About 190 megawatts of that is new capacity: an uprate, which raises the output of an existing reactor with new equipment, expected online between 2030 and 2032. Constellation put the associated Maryland infrastructure investment above 3 billion dollars. The plant's power keeps flowing into the PJM grid; Amazon buys a share of output, not a private wire.

Six days later, on October 6, Constellation and Google announced the larger version of the same play: a 20 year agreement supporting 890 megawatts of new nuclear output from uprates at 11 existing units across six sites in Illinois, Pennsylvania, and New Jersey, backed by more than 4.3 billion dollars of Constellation investment, with the first uprate expected by 2028 and the full 890 megawatts targeted before the end of 2032. A separate 15 year agreement covers 2,700 megawatts from Constellation's existing PJM fleet. That second figure is supply, not new capacity: only about a quarter of the package is new.

On October 15, Venture Global, a liquefied natural gas exporter, announced agreements with GE Vernova and Baker Hughes for equipment that could expand its private, gas fired power network by up to 16 power islands of 720 megawatts each, with deliveries running from 2028 to 2034. The company is considering data centers beside its existing industrial sites, with an ambition of up to 10 gigawatts of computing capacity by the end of 2034. That ambition is not a built business: it is a development target, and the equipment agreements are reservations and orders, not live server power. The pattern is global: on October 1, Japan's JERA, Dell Technologies, and RHAELM Holdings signed a memorandum of understanding for up to 400 megawatts of AI infrastructure at JERA's Chiba thermal power station, phased from 2028.

2. Why power, and why now

The wrong assumption to challenge is that compute demand automatically becomes compute supply. A data center campus needs three things at once: grid connection, generation, and permission to build, and the slowest of the three sets the schedule. PJM Interconnection, the grid operator covering 13 states, has proposed that large new loads either bring their own power or face disconnection during peak demand, and Construction Dive reported in October that PJM is short about 6.8 gigawatts of new capacity as data center demand rises. That is the context for Google's timing: the company signed before the region's data center power rules are final.

Uprates are attractive for exactly this reason. A new reactor takes a decade or more of licensing and construction, while an uprate reuses a licensed, grid connected site and adds output in increments, with the first Google increment targeted for 2028. Gas fired power islands play the same role from the other side: a fuel supplier that already owns gas, land, and industrial sites can offer power without waiting in a grid queue. In both cases the seller is selling a delivery date, which is the one thing a chip order cannot provide.

3. What this changes about where value accrues

First, the bottleneck shifts bargaining power. When accelerators were scarce, chipmakers set terms. When megawatts with a delivery date are scarce, utilities, gas suppliers, and factory builders set terms, and hyperscalers sign 15 to 20 year contracts to lock them in. Constellation is the counterparty on all three defining deals: Microsoft's Three Mile Island restart in 2024, Amazon's Calvert Cliffs agreement, and Google's uprate program.

Second, site selection follows power, not the other way around. A campus beside a gas export terminal or an uprated reactor fleet has a location chosen by the power answer. Regions that can offer firm power quickly will attract capacity, while regions with long interconnection queues will lose projects regardless of their chip supply.

Third, the headline numbers will keep misleading. Every figure needs three questions: new capacity or existing supply, contract, reservation, or built asset, and what date is committed. The Google package is 890 megawatts new, not 3,590. The Venture Global figure is up to 11.52 gigawatts of possible generation nameplate, against a 10 gigawatt compute ambition, against zero gigawatts yet delivered. The JERA figure is a memorandum covering a full capital stack, not a construction start.

Three things to watch:

  • Whether PJM finalizes its bring your own power rules, and whether other grid operators copy them.
  • Whether the first Constellation uprate lands in 2028, and whether each unit clears regulatory approval on schedule.
  • Whether factory built delivery closes the schedule gap in practice, tested against Polar Data Centers' 41 megawatt first phase at Heroya, Norway.

The chip race is not over. It has met a constraint it cannot solve alone: the next AI race will be won by whoever can put a megawatt and a finished hall on the same date.
