Why Are Classical Computers Now the Deciding Factor in the Quantum Race?

IBM's Nighthawk r3 and PsiQuantum's AMD pact put decoders, interconnects and HPC at the centre

16 Nov 2026 · By The Signal

In six days, quantum computing had its busiest week of the year, and almost none of the news was about qubits alone. On November 6, IBM switched on a Quantum System Two in Chicago. On November 11, it announced Nighthawk r3, a processor designed for 10,000 quantum operations across 520 programmable qubits. On November 12, PsiQuantum and AMD expanded their partnership to co-design the classical infrastructure around fault-tolerant photonic machines. Three announcements, three different companies, one shared message: the race is no longer decided inside the quantum chip. It is decided by the classical computers wrapped around it.

The numbers behind the IBM announcement explain why. Nighthawk r2, introduced in September, delivers up to 100,000 samples per second and supports accurate computations with more than 7,500 quantum operations on 120 qubits. Partners including E.ON, Algorithmiq and Q-CTRL have run more than 34 billion circuits on it, reporting speedups of up to 19 times over previous IBM processors. E.ON reported a 15 times speedup in quantum machine learning for energy grid optimisation. An IBM experiment with Oak Ridge National Laboratory simulated real magnetic materials 12 times faster than before, with results consistent with neutron scattering experiments. Speed, not qubit count, is the metric IBM chose to lead with, because a quantum computer returns its answer one sample at a time, and useful workloads need millions or billions of them.

Nighthawk r3 extends that architecture rather than replacing it: 520 programmable qubits out of 2,034 physical qubits, designed for 10,000 operations, and ten chip-to-chip interconnects called l-couplers. In 2027 IBM plans to link two r3 processors into a single 1,040-qubit system. The enabling result is already measured: IBM connected Nighthawk r1 processors with four l-couplers at 99.3 percent quantum state transfer fidelity, against a 99.9 percent target, and joined two cryogenic modules into one system that reached operating temperature in under five days and cooled below 15 millikelvin. The honest reading matters here. The r3 figures are design targets, not measured performance from finished hardware. The 1,040-qubit system is a plan for 2027, not a machine that exists today. What is measured is the interconnect, and the interconnect is the point: IBM is scaling by joining chips, not by building one ever larger chip.

The decoder is the new bottleneck

PsiQuantum and AMD made the same point from the photonic side. Their expanded partnership, announced November 12, adds AMD Instinct GPUs, EPYC CPUs and the ROCm software stack to an existing relationship built on Versal programmable chips, and AMD plans an equity investment of undisclosed size. The technical core is PRISM, a machine learning decoder for error correction that researchers from both companies described in a preprint posted November 10. Run on an AMD VP1902 FPGA, it decoded distance-8 logical blocks in 6.59 microseconds. That number is narrow, a benchmark for one decoder on one workload, not the speed of a quantum computer, and the paper is a preprint, not peer-reviewed work. But it shows where the engineering effort now sits: a fault-tolerant machine must detect and correct errors in real time, millions of times a second, and that job falls entirely on classical hardware sitting centimetres from the qubits.

Chicago is where the two halves meet

The geography underlines it. IBM's System Two in Chicago runs Nighthawk r2 at Hyde Park Labs and will move to the Illinois Quantum and Microelectronics Park, where it will support the National Quantum Algorithm Center. PsiQuantum is the anchor tenant of the same park, and its AMD partnership may expand there too. IBM has also co-located Nighthawk with Oak Ridge National Laboratory's Umbra supercomputer, powered by AMD Instinct MI355X GPUs and 5th Gen EPYC CPUs, as a co-design platform for error correction. The pattern is consistent: quantum processors are being installed next to supercomputers, not instead of them.

What to watch, and what to ignore

Three tests will show whether this week was a turning point or a press release cycle. First, whether IBM's l-coupler fidelity climbs from 99.3 percent toward its 99.9 percent target, because every crossing of that link loses quantum information, and a two-chip system will cross it constantly. Second, whether PRISM-style decoders hold their latency as code distances grow, since a decoder that is fast at distance 8 and slow at distance 15 solves nothing. Third, whether the unified IBM Quantum Platform delivers the workflow gains IBM reports, such as the sample-based quantum diagonalization workflow run with RIKEN five times faster while doing 100 times more computations, or Cleveland Clinic and RIKEN processing 608 million samples 33 times faster with automation. Ignore the qubit counts in isolation. A 1,040-qubit system with slow decoding and lossy interconnects is a bigger machine that still cannot finish a useful calculation. A smaller machine that samples fast, decodes in microseconds and plugs straight into a supercomputer is closer to useful work.

That is the shift this week marks. For a decade the quantum race was scored by counting qubits. The companies now leading it are scoring it by samples per second, decoder latency and interconnect fidelity, and they are hiring chipmakers and supercomputer builders to win on those numbers. The qubit is still the star of the announcement. The classical computer next to it is doing more of the work.
