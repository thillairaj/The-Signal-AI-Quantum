In August 2026, the IETF published RFC 10024, standardizing hybrid post-quantum key agreement for TLS 1.3. It defines three key-exchange groups: X25519MLKEM768, SecP256r1MLKEM768, and SecP384r1MLKEM1024. Each runs classical elliptic-curve key agreement alongside ML-KEM, the lattice-based key-encapsulation mechanism NIST standardized as FIPS 203 in August 2024. Two key agreements run inside one handshake, and the session stays safe as long as either one holds.

How the handshake works

When both sides support the hybrid group, the client runs a classical X25519 Diffie-Hellman exchange and, in parallel, an ML-KEM-768 encapsulation. The two secrets are combined into the TLS 1.3 key schedule. An attacker must break both algorithms to recover the session key: a quantum computer that defeats X25519 still faces the lattice problem, and a classical lattice breakthrough still faces X25519. The redundancy is not theoretical caution. In July 2026, AI-assisted cryptanalysis found a structural weakness in the HAWK signature candidate, and the scheme was withdrawn within days. New mathematics carries real uncertainty, and the hybrid keeps classical cryptography as the backstop.

The motive is the harvest now, decrypt later attack: encrypted traffic recorded today can be decrypted by a future quantum computer, so key exchange is the one part of TLS that must be quantum resistant before that machine exists. It is also why the migration stops at key exchange for now. Certificate signatures remain classical, because forging one would need a quantum computer at the moment of the handshake, and recorded traffic gives an attacker nothing to forge with.

Why it matters

Deployment is nearly invisible. On October 15, 2026, the UK's National Cyber Security Centre issued updated TLS guidance recommending hybrid ML-KEM key exchange, and it is already the default in Chrome, Edge, and Firefox. Imperva switched it on across its network with no measurable performance penalty, and Cloudflare's September 2026 figures put post-quantum hybrid traffic at roughly 70% of its browser connections. The operator's action item is short: run a current TLS library, OpenSSL 3.5 or later carries X25519MLKEM768 by default, and confirm both legs of every connection can negotiate it.
