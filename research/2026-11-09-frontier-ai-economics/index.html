Why Does Frontier AI Cost More the Faster It Grows?

Anthropic's S-1 puts the frontier lab business model on the public record: $17 billion in a quarter, and a $895 billion compute bill behind it

9 Nov 2026 · By The Signal

Signal Research Notes is an original-research series on The Signal, published three times a week. Each edition asks one question, works through it from primary sources, and keeps confirmed facts separate from analysis. Sources are linked at the end.

On November 2, 2026, Anthropic filed an amended IPO prospectus with the US Securities and Exchange Commission, and one quarter of numbers changed the story of the frontier AI business. Revenue in the three months to September 30 was $16,956 million, about $17 billion, up 1,127 percent on a year earlier and up 47 percent on the previous quarter. Nine-month revenue reached $33,242 million. By any normal measure this is a company printing money. It also reported a $567 million operating loss in the same quarter, after a $974 million operating profit in the quarter before. Growth made it poorer, and the filing explains exactly why.

The compute line is eating the revenue line

Compute and infrastructure expenses were $9,863 million in the quarter, about $10 billion, or 58 percent of revenue. A quarter earlier the same line was $5,558 million, 48 percent of revenue. Revenue grew 47 percent quarter on quarter; the compute bill grew 77 percent. Revenue generated per dollar spent on compute fell from just over two times to 1.72 times. Anthropic says it expects compute and infrastructure expenses to keep rising in absolute terms and to remain its largest operating expense for the foreseeable future.

The net loss of $23,291 million needs a qualifier. It includes a $21.9 billion non-cash loss tied to changes in the fair value of convertible notes, derivative liabilities and other securities, which the filing links to the company's rising valuation. That is an accounting entry, not cash out of the door. The operating loss is the cleaner signal, and it swung by more than $1.5 billion in one quarter because compute costs rose faster than sales.

A $895 billion promise, bought in advance

The largest number in the filing is not a cost of this quarter. Anthropic discloses purchase commitments and lease agreements totalling about $895.4 billion, substantially all of it related to compute and infrastructure. About $448 billion of that is expected to be incurred before the end of 2030, and about $69.8 billion falls due in the twelve months after September 30, 2026. The Google cloud agreement alone carries a minimum commitment of about $121.3 billion from April 2026 to July 2033.

This is the fixed-cost asymmetry at the heart of the frontier model business. Training a frontier model needs secured capacity over years-long horizons, so the capacity is bought before the revenue exists. The revenue, meanwhile, is consumption-based and carries no customer minimums. When demand surges past the secured capacity, the lab buys short-term compute at a premium. In the third quarter Anthropic bought $5.0 billion of compute under short-term commitments, which the filing says costs significantly more than capacity secured through multi-year agreements. It has been paying SpaceX $1.25 billion a month for compute and has signed multi-billion-dollar deals with neocloud providers Nscale, Akamai and Volta.

The squeeze comes from the other side too

While the compute bill climbs, the price of intelligence falls. Constant-capability inference has deflated roughly ten times a year for four consecutive years: GPT-3-class output cost $60 per million tokens in November 2021 and $0.06 by late 2024, and GPT-4-class pricing is down about 300 times since March 2023. A lab sells a product whose unit price collapses every year, and buys the capacity to make it under multi-year fixed commitments. That is a business model with leverage in both directions.

The response is visible at both rival labs. On November 3, OpenAI began routing parts of its always-on dots agents to GPT-6.1 Terra, which it says matches the flagship GPT-6 Astra on Agents' Last Exam at about a tenth of the per-token price. Two days earlier, Elon Musk said Grok Bot, which had 800,000 paid weekly users in early October, cost more to serve than it earned. Flat subscriptions for round-the-clock agents break the pricing model built for chatbots, and both companies are moving toward metered work and cheaper models underneath.

What actually protects the margin

The wrong assumption is that scale fixes this. Anthropic's filing shows scale doing the opposite while capacity is bought short: every new dollar of revenue arrived with more than a dollar of new compute cost behind it in the quarter. What protects the margin is not more volume. It is cheaper capacity and cheaper routing. Multi-year agreements cost less per unit than short-term buys, which is why Anthropic is building its own compute base through a project called Anthropic Direct. Routing work to the cheapest model that can do it, the Terra move, is the same logic applied to the output side.

Two numbers will tell whether the third quarter was a spike or a structure. First, the compute share of revenue: if it falls back toward 48 percent as multi-year capacity comes online, the squeeze was a capacity timing problem. Second, the IPO roadshow itself: the price range and share count are still blank in the filing, and investors will price the company on whether they believe $895 billion of fixed commitments can be serviced by consumption revenue that carries no minimums. The frontier AI business is no longer a question of whether the models work. It is a question of whether the arithmetic works, and for the first time the arithmetic is on the public record.
