Six things a founder should hold on to

Retention before acquisition

A product that keeps users grows from a compounding base. A product that leaks them must rebuy its growth every month at auction prices. Fix the bucket before turning up the tap.

Growth is loops, not hacks

Campaigns end; loops keep turning. The durable question for any growth idea is whether its output becomes the input of the next cycle: users making content, invitations, integrations or data that bring the next users.

One North Star, honestly measured

Pick the metric that represents value delivered, review it weekly, and audit its definition yearly. A metric the team can game will be gamed, usually without anyone meaning harm.

Cohorts tell the truth

Blended averages hide decay. Cohort retention and growth accounting show whether the product is actually improving or whether growth is just more new users covering the same old churn.

Efficiency is a growth metric

Burn multiple, the Rule of 40 and default-alive status belong on the same dashboard as growth rate. Growth that cannot survive the next funding winter is a loan, not an achievement.

Frameworks are lenses

AARRR, ICE, RICE and the rest organise thinking; they do not replace knowing your customer. Use them to structure experiments, then believe your own data over any framework, including the ones in this table.

A founder's growth checklist

  • Can you state your ICP and beachhead segment in one sentence each?
  • Have you run the Sean Ellis PMF survey in the last two quarters?
  • Is your North Star metric named, defined and reviewed weekly?
  • Do you read retention by cohort every month, not just in aggregate?
  • Do you know your activation rate and your time to value?
  • Is at least one growth experiment running every single week?
  • Do you know your blended CAC, LTV and CAC payback by channel?
  • Have you computed the burn multiple for the last quarter?
  • Are you default alive today, on paper, with no next fundraise assumed?
  • Is expansion revenue measured separately from new-customer revenue?

Worked example: is your virality an engine?

4 invites per user x 25% acceptance = K of 1.0

Suppose each user sends 4 invitations on average, and 1 in 4 invitees becomes an active user. K = 4 x 0.25 = 1.0, the break-even point of viral growth: every user exactly replaces themselves.

At 2 invites and the same 25% acceptance, K is 0.5. That is not an engine, but it still halves the new users your paid channels must buy. Both numbers are useful; only one of them is magic.

Educational only. Growth frameworks are lenses for thinking, not guarantees; every tactic here has to be validated against your own market, product and data before it deserves budget.
