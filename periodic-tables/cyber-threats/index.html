Six things a leader should hold on to

Attacks are chains

Every serious incident passes through stages: research, entry, persistence, movement, impact. Breaking any single link early collapses the whole attack, which is why stage-by-stage thinking beats tool collecting.

Entry is usually ordinary

Most intrusions begin with a phished password, an unpatched public system or a trusted supplier, not exotic hacking. Boring basics, done relentlessly, close the doors attackers actually use.

Dwell time decides damage

Attackers are usually inside for days or weeks before anything visible happens. The quiet period is your opportunity: measure how fast you detect and evict, and drive that number down.

Identity is the new perimeter

Escalation and lateral movement run on stolen credentials and excess privilege. MFA, least privilege and protected admin accounts break more attack chains than any perimeter upgrade.

Backups are a target, not a given

Competent attackers delete backups and stop services before detonating. Isolated, immutable, restore-tested backups are the difference between an incident and an existential crisis.

Process beats panic

BEC, deepfake fraud and extortion all exploit rushed human decisions. Verification steps that cannot be skipped, even for the CEO, quietly prevent the largest losses.

A leader's threat-readiness checklist

Know your exposure

  • Current inventory of internet-facing systems, each with a named owner
  • Patch times for public systems measured in hours and days
  • MFA on email, VPN, cloud consoles and finance systems, with no silent exceptions
  • Critical suppliers named in the risk register, with their security questioned
  • Logs shipped off devices to a protected central store

Be ready to respond

  • Backups isolated and immutable, with full restores actually tested
  • An incident plan with named decision-makers, legal counsel and communications drafts
  • Payment-change and payment-approval verification that no seniority can bypass
  • Tabletop exercises run with executives, including a ransomware and a deepfake scenario
  • Detection metrics reviewed regularly: time to detect, time to contain

The honest assumption

Plan on the assumption that an attacker will get in eventually — through a person, a patch gap or a supplier. That is not pessimism; it is the design brief. Organisations that assume breach detect faster, lose less and recover in days instead of months, because the rehearsal happened before the deadline did.
