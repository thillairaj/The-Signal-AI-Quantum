The Leadership Periodic Table

Leadership Keywords

The working vocabulary of leadership — the behaviours, decisions and conversations that determine whether people trust, perform and grow — organised as a periodic table.

Core principle: Leadership is not a title, a corner office or a personality trait; it is a practice of repeatable behaviours, most of them small and daily. These terms name those behaviours precisely, because a leader can only improve what they can name. Read the table as a mirror: the tiles you avoid are usually the ones your team needs most.

Six things to hold on to

Leadership is behaviour, not position

People follow what you repeatedly do — what you attend to, reward, tolerate and finish. The title only rents attention; behaviour is what converts it into trust.

Trust is the operating system

Credibility, reliability and low self-orientation compound quietly. Every kept promise is a deposit; every gap between words and action is a withdrawal the whole team notices.

Clarity is a kindness

Clear priorities, clear ownership and clear feedback respect people's time and dignity. Vagueness feels gentle to the leader and is experienced as anxiety by the team.

Safety and standards together

Psychological safety without standards becomes comfort; standards without safety become silence. The learning zone — where performance actually grows — needs both at once.

Decide at the right speed

Slow down for the irreversible, speed up for the reversible, and record why. Most leadership time is wasted by applying the wrong speed to the wrong class of decision.

You are the ceiling

Your self-awareness, energy and willingness to grow set the limit for your team's. Development is not a reward for spare time; it is maintenance on the instrument everything else runs through.

A weekly leadership practice checklist

  • Hold every one-on-one, and let the agenda be theirs
  • Give one piece of specific feedback close to the event, and one genuine recognition
  • Restate the top three priorities and say no to one thing that competes with them
  • Review your commitments list and close or renegotiate anything slipping
  • Ask one question you genuinely do not know the answer to, in the room
  • Protect two blocks of deep work, and defend your team's as visibly as your own
  • Bring one piece of bad news upward early, with a plan attached
  • Put one person's name forward in a room they cannot access
  • Check workload reality on your most committed person, including yourself
  • Write one lesson from the week and the rule you will test from it

A note on using this table

No leader is strong on all 62 tiles, and the table is not a scorecard. Pick two or three tiles a quarter — ideally the ones your team would pick for you — and practise them deliberately until the behaviour is boring. That is how every tile on this table was ever built.
