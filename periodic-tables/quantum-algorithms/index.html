Core principle: Only a few dozen quantum algorithm families have credible speedups, and each one works by exploiting interference or problem structure that classical machines cannot use in the same way. The discipline of this field is separating proven complexity results from demonstrations and marketing: an algorithm is a resource claim, not a press release.

Six things to hold on to

Structure is the speedup

Every exponential quantum speedup exploits structure — periodicity, algebraic structure, the physics of a quantum system itself. Unstructured problems get at most a quadratic Grover gain. When you evaluate a claim, name the structure being exploited or discount it.

Query complexity is not runtime

Oracle separations are real theorems about an idealised model. End-to-end advantage must also pay for building the oracle, loading the data and reading the answer — the three places famous speedups quietly die.

Two eras, two algorithm families

Variational and mitigation-based methods are the NISQ era: bounded, noisy, sampling-hungry. Phase estimation, Shor and qubitisation are the fault-tolerant era: proven and powerful, waiting on logical qubits. Confusing the two eras is the most common error in quantum commentary.

Resource estimates are the ground truth

Logical qubits, T-count, physical-qubit footprint and runtime, under stated error assumptions — that is what a serious algorithm proposal looks like. The field can now cost its flagship applications; insist that claims use the same accounting.

Classical algorithms fight back

Dequantisation, tensor networks and better heuristics have retired many quantum claims after publication. A speedup that survives years of classical counterattack is a different class of result from a launch announcement.

Simulation is the surest ground

Quantum simulation of quantum systems — chemistry and materials — rests on the firmest theoretical footing, because the exponential classical cost is intrinsic, not an artefact of problem encoding. Expect the first unambiguous, useful advantages there.

A realism checklist for algorithm claims

Questions for any speedup claim

  • Is the speedup exponential, polynomial or quadratic — and proven, or benchmarked?
  • Is the result in the oracle model? What does the oracle cost to build?
  • How is classical data loaded, and what does state preparation cost end to end?
  • How is the answer read out? Does readout cost scale with the system size?
  • Which classical algorithm is the baseline — and is it the best known?

Questions for any demonstration

  • Does it need fault tolerance? If so, what are the logical-qubit and T-count estimates?
  • What is the total shot count or sampling overhead, including mitigation and optimiser loops?
  • Could a tensor network, stabiliser simulation or dequantised algorithm reproduce it?
  • Is the problem instance classically verifiable only because it is small?
  • Has the claim survived independent attempts to match it classically?

The honest summary

Most of the powerful algorithms in this table — Shor, phase estimation, qubitised chemistry — assume fault-tolerant hardware that does not yet exist, and the near-term family has not yet beaten strong classical baselines on useful problems. That is not a reason for dismissal; it is the accurate map. Quantum computing's proven mathematics is strong, its engineering frontier is logical qubits, and its first durable advantages are most likely in simulation. Invest attention in proportion to evidence, and let resource estimates — not announcements — set the pace.
